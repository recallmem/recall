How Recall compares

Recall next to other memory systems, and next to the model alone reading the whole history with no memory system at all. Each chart first lists the systems graded the same way as Recall (LongMemEval-S's GPT-4o judge; BEAM's own scoring), whose numbers compare directly, then every other published figure, which used other judges, answer models or question sets. Hover a bar, or open the table under a chart, for its answer model, judge and question count; every figure links to where it was published.

BEAM 100K

BEAM (ICLR 2026) asks 400 questions about 20 long conversations across ten abilities, from recalling a detail to ordering events, resolving contradictions and knowing when to say "I don't know". We ran all 400 questions, 20 conversations of about 125,000 tokens against the hosted service, with Recall Lumen answering, Recall Loom building memory, and one model call per answer. The scoring is BEAM's own evaluation code, ported from the BEAM repository (commit b2da22e) with its judge prompts unchanged: each rubric item of a question is judged on its own and the question scores their mean, and event ordering scores Kendall tau-b, normalised to 0-1, after the judge matches the answer's items to the reference events. The judge is gpt-4.1-mini at temperature 0, the model BEAM's code uses. All 400 questions were scored.

BEAM 500K

The same benchmark with conversations five times longer, about 500,000 tokens each. We ran all 35 of its conversations, 20 questions each, with the same models (Recall Lumen answers, Recall Loom builds memory) and the same scoring: BEAM's own evaluation code, graded by gpt-4.1-mini. All 700 questions were scored. Saying "I don't know" when the conversation never answered is the strongest ability at this length; ordering events and summarizing are again the weakest. The best system in the BEAM paper scores 0.359 at this length.

LongMemEval-S

LongMemEval-S gives each question its own chat history of about 50 sessions and 100,000 tokens, then asks about it: facts the user or the assistant mentioned, preferences, updates, dates, and things that were never said. We ran all 500 questions, one workspace each, with Recall Lumen answering and Recall Loom building memory. The answers are graded by the benchmark's official judge, gpt-4o-2024-08-06 with the prompts from LongMemEval's own evaluation script, the judge most published LongMemEval-S figures report; that makes this figure more comparable than our earlier sample, which our own rubric graded. Recall answered 455 of 500 correctly (95% interval 88.2-93.2%). Two questions were never asked because a background memory job failed in each workspace (a provider error), and they count as wrong. The weakest types are questions that combine several sessions (102 of 121) and preferences (26 of 30); questions about what the assistant itself said are among the strongest (54 of 56).

LoCoMo

On 75 held-out LoCoMo questions (15 per conversation: single-hop, multi-hop, temporal and open-domain), self-hosted with Recall Loom for every task and judged by gpt-4.1, Recall answered 60.0% correctly under a strict judge and 79.2% under a lenient one, with a median answer time of 1.6 s. Published LoCoMo scores are usually higher because of lenient judges and an easier question mix.

Method

  • System under test. The hosted service at api.recallmem.dev, exactly as customers use it: messages go in through the API, background reasoning runs, then each question is asked through chat at the default low level. The memory for these runs was built on 10 October 2026 and every question asked on 11 October.
  • Models. Answers by Recall Lumen; memory (reasoning over messages, summaries) built by Recall Loom; embeddings by Recall Embed. These are the names of the models the hosted service runs.
  • Scoring. BEAM is scored with BEAM's own evaluation code (protocol beam-ref-b2da22e: ported from the BEAM repository at commit b2da22e, with its judge prompts verbatim). Each rubric item is judged 0, 0.5 or 1 with BEAM's judge prompt and a question scores the mean of its items; event ordering scores Kendall tau-b normalised as (tau + 1) / 2, after the judge aligns the answer's items with the reference events; an ability is the mean of its questions and the overall score the mean of the ten abilities. The judge is openai/gpt-4.1-mini at temperature 0, the model BEAM's code uses, and every question was scored (400 at 100K, 700 at 500K). LongMemEval-S uses the benchmark's official judge, gpt-4o-2024-08-06 with the prompts from its evaluate_qa.py; an answer counts as correct when the judge says yes.
  • Isolation. One workspace per BEAM conversation and per LongMemEval-S question, so no memory is shared between them.
  • Between runs. The answering rules follow general lessons we wrote down after reading earlier runs' mistakes (name conflicting statements with their dates, say "I don't know" when the record is silent, read the conversation log for overview questions). Nothing is specific to a question or an answer.
  • Single runs. Each figure is one run, with no repeats. The 95% intervals are 0.653-0.713 on BEAM 100K, 0.643-0.695 on BEAM 500K and 88.2-93.2% on LongMemEval-S, and wider per ability or type.

What's next

Event ordering and summaries on BEAM, and LongMemEval-S questions that span several sessions, are what we are improving now. The 1M split follows. New results land on this page, including the ones that do not flatter us.
